## Spaced Repetition: The Science Behind Why Diphthong Dash Works

You've probably experienced this: You learn someone's name at a party, and two hours later, you've forgotten it.

But introduce yourself five times over a month, and you never forget.

That's spaced repetition at work — and it's one of the most powerful learning principles neuroscience has discovered.

### The Forgetting Curve

In 1885, psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus did a simple experiment. He memorized lists of nonsense syllables and tested how quickly he forgot them.

His finding: **We forget most of what we learn within hours.**

But here's the key: Each time he *reviewed* the material before forgetting it completely, the forgetting curve flattened. He forgot more slowly.

**The pattern:**
- 1st exposure: Forget after 1 hour
- 1st review (before forgetting): Forget after 1 day
- 2nd review (before forgetting): Forget after 1 week
- 3rd review (before forgetting): Forget after 1 month

Each retrieval (each time you recall something) makes the memory stronger and more durable.

### Why This Matters for Phonics

Reading is pattern recognition. Your child sees the letters "oa" and needs to automatically know it sounds like "oh."

That automaticity doesn't come from seeing "oa" ten times in one day. It comes from seeing it, forgetting it slightly, and being reminded of it *just in time*.

Here's why:

**Memory has two components:**
1. **Strength** — How well you remember it right now
2. **Durability** — How long the memory lasts

When you cram (study the same thing repeatedly in one sitting), you increase strength temporarily. But durability stays low. Forget for a few days, and you've lost it.

When you space practice, you increase durability. The memory becomes part of long-term storage.

For reading, durability is everything. Your child doesn't need to *remember* that "oa" sounds like "oh" *right now*. They need to know it automatically *three months from now* when they encounter "float" in a novel.

### The Research: Spaced Repetition Works

Decades of research confirm this. A meta-analysis by Dunlosky et al. (2013) ranked learning strategies by effectiveness. Spaced practice ranked near the top.

**Key finding:** Spaced practice is more effective for:
- Long-term retention
- Transfer to new contexts
- Automatic recall (no thinking required)

Exactly what reading fluency needs.

Studies on phonics specifically show:
- Students receiving spaced vowel-team practice master patterns 2-3 weeks faster
- They retain patterns longer
- They apply patterns to unfamiliar words independently

### How Spacing Works: The Optimal Gap

The question becomes: *When should you review?*

**Too soon:** You haven't forgotten enough. The memory doesn't strengthen.  
**Too late:** You've forgotten completely. You have to relearn from scratch.

**Just right:** You're about to forget, but remember it with a small effort.

Research suggests optimal spacing follows this pattern:
- 1st exposure
- Review after 1 day
- Review after 3 days
- Review after 1 week
- Review after 2 weeks
- Review after 1 month

But the exact timing varies by learner and material. Some kids need more frequent reviews. Some can go longer between reviews.

**This is why personalized spacing works better than fixed schedules.** And this is why algorithms beat worksheets.

### Algorithms vs. Worksheets

**Worksheet approach:**
- Monday: Learn 'oa' words (boat, coat, goat, float, toad, load, toast)
- Practice all 7 words multiple times
- Move on to next pattern

**Problem:** The child sees all 'oa' words on the same day. They memorize the list. But they haven't built durability. Two weeks later, they've forgotten.

**Algorithm approach (spaced repetition):**
- Day 1: Show 'oa' word (boat). Child gets it right.
- Day 2: Show different 'oa' word (coat). Child gets it right.
- Day 3: Show 'oa' word again (boat). Child hesitates. *Just right.*
- Day 4: Mix 'oa' with new patterns. Child gets it right.
- Day 8: Show 'oa' word (float). Child gets it right.
- Day 15: Show 'oa' with other patterns. Child gets it right.

The algorithm tracks when the child last saw each pattern and shows it again *just before forgetting*.

This is why spaced-repetition apps outperform worksheets in studies. The app doesn't just store knowledge; it protects it from decay.

### Retrieval Practice: The Other Half of the Equation

Spaced repetition only works if there's **retrieval practice** — the child actually has to *retrieve* the memory, not just recognize it.

**Recognition vs. retrieval:**
- **Recognition:** "Is this the right answer?" (multiple choice)
- **Retrieval:** "What is this word?" (open-ended)

Retrieval is harder, but it creates stronger memories.

Good phonics games use retrieval:
- "What word is this?" (show letters, child has to retrieve the sound and decode)
- Not: "Is this boat or goat?" (recognition, easier but weaker)

### Motivation and Spacing

Here's where games become valuable.

Worksheets make spacing *boring*. Kids don't want to see the same pattern again three days later. It feels repetitive.

Games make spacing *engaging*. The same pattern appears, but in a different context, with different words, and with scoring or achievement feedback.

The child doesn't feel like they're doing the same thing. They feel like they're making progress.

This is the hidden genius of game-based learning: It makes necessary repetition feel fresh.

### Practical Takeaway for Parents

If your child is learning vowel teams, the ideal learning sequence is:

1. **Introduction** — Explicit teaching from teacher or parent
2. **Immediate practice** — Same day, multiple examples
3. **Short review** — 1-2 days later
4. **Medium review** — 1 week later
5. **Long review** — 2-4 weeks later
6. **Integration** — Mix with other patterns

Each review should be brief (5-10 minutes) but focused.

If your child's school only does step 1 and 2, supplemental practice at home filling in steps 3-6 makes a massive difference.
