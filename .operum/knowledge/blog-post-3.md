## Heart Words vs. Decodable: A Teacher's Guide to Teaching Sight Words

The term "sight word" makes teachers wince. For decades, the instruction has been: *"Just memorize these 220 words. They don't follow phonics rules."*

Not only is this demotivating for students. It's also wrong.

The distinction between true sight words and "heart words" (or "tricky words") changes how we teach them — and dramatically improves fluency.

### Terminology Matters

**Sight words** — Words that appear frequently in text and should be recognized automatically on sight.

Not all sight words are the same:
- Some *do* follow phonics rules but are simply common (the, that, with)
- Some *don't* follow rules (was, said, come) — these are "heart words"

The difference matters for instruction.

### The Traditional Approach (And Why It Fails)

Traditional sight-word lists (Dolch, Fry, etc.) lump both types together:

"Memorize these words: the, that, was, said, come, have, where..."

Students are told: "These don't follow rules. Just memorize them."

**The problem:**
- Students get demoralized ("I'm bad at reading")
- Teachers waste time drilling unmemorable words
- Students don't develop pattern recognition
- Transfer to new contexts is poor

### Decodable Words vs. Heart Words

Let me separate them:

#### **Decodable Words** (Teaching Opportunity)

These *do* follow phonics rules. They're in sight-word lists because they're frequent.

**Examples:**
- **the** — We don't say "thuh." We say "thuh" or "thee." This is a *pronunciation* convention, not a phonics rule. But it's decodable once you know: *e* can say "uh."
- **that** — *th-a-t*. All phonetically regular.
- **with** — *w-i-th*. All phonetically regular.
- **have** — *h-a-v-e*. *e* at the end says "uh." Regular.

**Teaching approach:** Don't say "memorize." Say "This word follows a pattern you know. Let's decode it." Students learn they *can* decode, which builds confidence.

#### **Heart Words** (Memory Work)

These *don't* follow standard phonics rules. They require true memorization.

**Examples:**
- **was** — We'd expect "waz" or "wuz," but it's "wuz." Irregular.
- **said** — We'd expect "sed," but it's "sed" spelled s-a-i-d. Irregular.
- **come** — We'd expect "kohm," but it's "cum." Irregular.
- **where** — Should sound like "wear," but sometimes "ware." Irregular.

These need explicit, intentional memorization.

### Dyslexia Connection: Why This Matters

Kids with dyslexia and reading struggles often have trouble with *automaticity* — the ability to recognize words instantly without conscious decoding.

**Here's the insight:**
- Decodable words can be automated through pattern recognition (easier)
- Heart words must be automated through memory (harder)

If we teach *all* sight words as pure memory drills, we're making it equally hard for struggling readers to learn both types.

**Better approach:**
1. Teach decodable words as *patterns* (leverages phonics knowledge)
2. Teach heart words as *special cases* (focuses memory on the exceptions)

Students with weak phonics struggle less if we separate these. Pattern learners can use their strength. Memory learners focus only on what must be memorized.

### How to Teach Heart Words Effectively

Heart words need **deliberate teaching and repeated exposure**.

#### **Step 1: Isolate the Irregular Part**

Don't make the student memorize the whole word as random.

**Example with "said":**
- "This word is 'said.'"
- "The first part 's' sounds like 's'." (regular)
- "The tricky part is 'a-i-d' — we say 'ed.'" (irregular)
- "Most words with 'a-i' say 'ay' like 'rain.' But 'said' is special. It says 'ed.'"

Now the student knows which part is irregular. Memory task is smaller.

#### **Step 2: Use Multiple Exposures Across Contexts**

Don't just drill the word on a worksheet.

**Week 1:**
- Read the word in a sentence
- Write the word
- Find it in a book

**Week 2:**
- Use it in a sentence
- Sentence dictation
- Short passage with multiple heart words

**Week 3:**
- Mix with other patterns
- Fluency practice
- Reading application

Spacing helps memory stick.

#### **Step 3: Anchor with Meaning and Imagery**

Heart words are easier to remember if anchored to meaning and visual memory.

**Example with "said":**
- "A character *said* something" — connect to story meaning
- Show picture/visual
- Use in context: "He said, 'Hello!'"

Memory for meaning supports memory for spelling.

### Decodable Text: The Overlooked Tool

Here's where decodable text becomes important.

**Decodable text** — Reading passages composed primarily of phonetically regular words, designed to practice phonics patterns.

Why teachers use them:
- Students can apply phonics patterns in context
- Builds automaticity with patterns
- Gives confidence ("I can read this!")

Why teachers hate them:
- Stories feel boring or artificial
- Limited vocabulary
- Students want "real" stories

**The truth:** Decodable text serves a purpose in the learning progression, but shouldn't *replace* real literature.

**Ideal model:**
1. Learn phonics patterns (isolated practice)
2. Apply patterns in decodable text (controlled practice)
3. Use patterns in real literature (authentic practice)
4. Build fluency through repeated reading of real stories

### The Leveled Reading Progression

Here's how to think about reading levels:

**Level 1: Pre-Phonics (Pre-K)**
- Picture books, rhyming texts
- Letter recognition

**Level 2: Early Phonics (K-1Q1)**
- Short vowels + initial consonants
- CVC words (cat, dog, sit)
- Decodable text focused on CVC

**Level 3: Vowel Teams (1Q2-2Q1)**
- Diphthongs and vowel teams
- Sight words that are decodable (the, that, with)
- Mix of decodable + real stories
- Introduction to heart words (was, said, have)

**Level 4: Complex Patterns (2Q2-3)**
- Blends, digraphs, silent letters
- Heart words mastered
- Mostly real literature with strong phonics base
- Fluency focus

**Level 5+: Advanced**
- Multi-syllabic words
- Morphology (prefixes, suffixes)
- Comprehension focus

Each level requires both:
- Explicit phonics instruction
- Supported reading practice (decodable transitioning to literature)
- Heart word mastery

### Classroom Implementation

If you're teaching reading, here's a practical framework:

#### **Explicit Phonics Block (20 minutes, daily)**
- Review previous patterns
- Teach new pattern
- Practice with word lists
- Apply to sentences

#### **Guided Reading (20-30 minutes)**
- Use decodable text for early levels
- Gradually transition to literature
- Support with pre-teaching vocabulary (especially heart words)

#### **Independent Reading (15-20 minutes)**
- "Goldilocks" books (not too hard, not too easy)
- Mix of practice and pleasure
- Track fluency

#### **Heart Word Focus (5-10 minutes, 3x weekly)**
- Spaced practice on heart words
- Flashcards, word walls, writing
- Integrated into daily reading

### Assessment

How do you know if students have mastered heart words vs. decodable words?

**Decodable assessment:** Can they decode new words with the pattern? (Phonics transfer test)

**Heart word assessment:** Can they recognize the word automatically in text without sounding out? (Fluency test)

If a student says "said" correctly but takes 2 seconds to recognize it, they're still working on automaticity. They need more spaced practice.

### The Big Picture

The traditional "memorize 220 sight words" approach wastes time on words students can figure out and lumps unmemorable heart words together.

**Better approach:**
1. Separate decodable words (teach as patterns) from heart words (teach as exceptions)
2. Use spaced repetition for both types
3. Progress from decodable text to literature
4. Assess for automaticity, not just recognition

Students become fluent readers faster, and they understand that reading *is* about patterns and rules — there are just a few memorable exceptions.
